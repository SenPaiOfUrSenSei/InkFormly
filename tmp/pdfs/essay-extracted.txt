My first hero was missing half his body.
On our old fourteen-inch Videocon, Spider-Man became “piderma,” villains vanished off the
edge of the screen, and half of the dialogue dissolved into static. I watched with biryani
balanced on my five-year-old lap, cheering anyway. Whenever someone asked what I wanted to
be, I shouted, “A hero!” To me, heroes were invincible superhumans who could help everyone.
So, whenever the movie turned from the mask to the vulnerable man beneath it, I turned back to
my biryani.
My first chance to play hero came not from a radioactive spider, but from three puppies in our
apartment’s parking lot. My sisters and I named them Blacky, Oreo, and Pie. We hid them
behind a cardboard box, away from our watchman uncle—who probably knew our secret
anyway. Every day, we brought milk and chapatis from home. Once, I even cooked the chapatis
myself—burnt at the edges and stiff as cardboard, though they seemed to love them anyway.
The puppies tumbled out, tails wagging, and I stayed until my mother called me upstairs.
I thought they would play with me forever. Why wouldn’t they?
One afternoon, only Oreo and Pie came running out. A strange smell lingered near the parking
lot, but I ignored it. The next night, restless, I sneaked downstairs and followed it to the open
gutter. Something small and black lay there, motionless.
It was trash, I told myself. It had to be.
The next morning, a rescue van came for Blacky’s body. Soon, it returned for Oreo’s. Within a
month, Pie was gone too.
I cried for three days and refused to eat. My mother switched on the Videocon, hoping to distract
me. Spider-Man was on. Peter held Uncle Ben as he died. My supposedly invincible hero
couldn't save someone he loved. Yet he kept running toward danger for strangers.
Why bother, if even he couldn't save everyone?
Then the power went out. I stared at the blank screen. Whenever our Videocon went black, the
picture eventually returned. Spider-Man would swing back into view, and his story would
continue.
For a while, I expected Blacky to come back, too.
For years, I wondered what those afternoons had amounted to. We fed the puppies, named
them, and hid them, yet none of it kept them safe. Sometimes, even now, I wish I could forget.
But I remember their wagging tails, the hours beside them, and the warmth of holding them in
my arms. I can't call those afternoons enough. But I can't call them meaningless, either.
Years later, I stopped at a grocery shop for my usual Pepsi. Outside, a street dog approached,
wagging his tail. I went back in for biscuits and milk. As he ate, I petted him, knowing I might
never see him again.
The old question hadn't disappeared. What was the point of caring for someone I couldn't keep
safe? I still couldn't say whether I'd done enough for Blacky, Oreo, and Pie. Perhaps I never
could. But I knew they had existed. I knew I had loved them. And I knew the dog in front of me
was hungry now.

I still want to fix things for the people I care about, even when I don't know how. When a
friend told me they were thinking of ending their life, I wanted to promise everything would
turn out all right. But I couldn't find a promise I knew I could keep. So I stayed on the
phone, listening long after I'd run out of things to say. I didn't know whether that would be
enough. I stayed anyway.

I think I understand a little more of the Spider-Man I used to ignore. He couldn't guarantee
anyone a happy ending. Neither can I.
I still want to be a hero. Only now, I stay for the parts of the story I used to skip.
